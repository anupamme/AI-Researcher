\subsection*{Ethics statement}
\label{sec:ethics}
\textbf{Human annotators.} One component of this work used human raters: three
annotators recruited via Prolific rated 100 AI-generated conversation transcripts
on five behavioural features (Appendix~\ref{app:human_icc_n100}). Annotators were
blind to ground-truth labels and to one another's ratings, and attention-check
items were embedded to verify engagement. They rated model-generated text only;
no personal or identifying data about any human was collected, presented or
stored. Annotators were paid at a rate of \$10 per hour, and gave their consent
before beginning the task. \textbf{No institutional review board review applied
to this work, and none was sought}; we state this rather than imply a safeguard
that was not in place. The study's per-rater CSVs are released.

\textbf{Model-generated content.} All deceptive content in our own experiments is
model output, elicited either by an explicit instruction to defend a false claim
or, in the criterion-4 materials, by presenting a knowledge-screened false
reference fact with no lie instruction at all. Claims are matched factual pairs
about neutral subject matter: science, history, geography, technology and culture
(Appendix~\ref{app:claims}), chosen so that a false claim is verifiable rather
than harmful. No experiment sought to elicit harmful, private or unsafe content,
and nothing here trains a model to deceive more effectively.

\textbf{Third-party data.} The two audited rollout releases are used under their
own terms. One is not redistributed: it is a git submodule pinned to a single
upstream commit. The other is access-gated, so we redistribute a sha256 manifest
and our derived artifacts rather than the corpus, and we accessed it under our
own account. Closed models were accessed via AWS Bedrock under standard terms.

\textbf{Dual use.} This paper is a critique of evaluation practice, not a
deception capability. It does report that much of a benchmark's accuracy is
reachable from surface features, and that the most discriminative probe in a
published battery is a paraphrase of the lie instruction, information that could
in principle help someone score well on such a benchmark without detecting
deception. We judge disclosure to be right: the failure mode is a property of the
benchmark design, published designs are already being used to support attribution
claims, and naming the mechanism is what allows it to be fixed. Every control we
describe is one an auditor can apply to a benchmark they did not build.

\textbf{Risk of over-reading a negative result.} The clearest misuse of this work
would be to cite it as evidence that LLM deception is undetectable, or to dismiss
deception evaluation as a whole. \textbf{We establish neither}, and say so in
§\ref{sec:eval_controls} and in claim~(4): under a design that removes the
elicitation confound, prior work's own battery does separate realized deception.
The claim is about what a class of benchmarks can license, not about what
detectors can do.

\subsection*{AI use statement}
Generative AI tools were used in preparing this work and we take full
responsibility for its final content. LLM assistance was used for writing and
editing the manuscript text, and for authoring and refactoring experiment code.
All references were independently verified against their authoritative records
(DOI, arXiv, or publisher metadata) using an automated citation-retrieval tool,
and the verification script is released with the code. All experimental results
reported here were produced by executed code against real model outputs, and
all numbers in the text were checked against the emitted result files. AI tools
were not used to generate data, results, or findings.

\subsection*{Reproducibility statement}
\textbf{The central result requires no closed-model API.} EXP-R1/R1b/R1c
(§\ref{sec:r1_faithful}) run against six open-weight targets served locally via
Ollama (Llama~3.2~3B, Llama~3.1~8B, Mistral~7B and Qwen~2.5~7B/14B/32B) with
fixed claim sets, fixed elicitation-question banks, and \emph{no} LLM feature
scoring: that detector is a logistic regression over binarised yes/no replies,
so no API-served model enters its pipeline at any stage.
The interrogation-pipeline experiments \emph{do} use a closed model: Claude
Haiku~4.5 is the interrogator and feature extractor throughout, via AWS
Bedrock, and Llama~3.1~8B, Llama~3.3~70B, Mistral Large~3 and Llama~4~Maverick
are Bedrock-served as targets, the remaining open-weight targets running locally
under Ollama (per-experiment infrastructure and cost in
Appendix~\ref{app:compute_cost}).
The protocol, the full 16-question elicitation bank, condition prompts, and
answer-parsing rules are given in Appendix~\ref{app:r1_protocol}; claim
construction is in Appendix~\ref{app:claims}; the interrogation pipeline is
specified in Appendix~\ref{app:adage_details}; and the regex patterns for the
label-free rule are in Appendix~\ref{app:regex_patterns}. The appendix opens with a
reader's guide (Table~\ref{tab:appendix_roadmap}) grouping its sections into five
buckets and naming the five that carry every load-bearing number. The external audits (§\ref{sec:external_audit},
Appendices~\ref{app:external_audit},~\ref{app:insider_audit},~\ref{app:external_audit_judge})
read a third-party corpus we do not redistribute: it is a git submodule pinned to
one upstream commit, fetched with a single \texttt{git submodule update --init},
after which every regex-arm audit result file regenerates byte-identically with no
model call and no further network access (\texttt{docs/EXTERNAL\_DATA.md}).
\textbf{EXP-XJ is the one audit that calls a model, and the detector is not ours}:
it is the corpus authors' own released black-box judge prompt
(\texttt{data/black\_box\_baseline/prompt.txt} in the pinned submodule, 1{,}373
characters, md5 \texttt{83ddc4bd2ebcc6c4b05baff091c5cc8c}), used verbatim and
unmodified, served through AWS Bedrock as
\texttt{us.anthropic.claude-haiku-4-5-20251001-v1:0} at temperature~0 with
\texttt{max\_tokens}~1000. Its 3{,}605 raw judgements are committed as JSONL, so
every EXP-XJ number in the paper recomputes from the repository with no model call
(Appendix~\ref{app:external_audit_judge}).
\textbf{EXP-WP (§\ref{sec:td_vs_fd}) needs weight access, and its activation
tensors are not a release artifact}: the probe reads residual hidden states from
local \texttt{Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507} weights under MPS at fp16 and from
\texttt{google/gemma-3-4b-it} at bf16 stored as float32 (Gemma-3's residual stream
carries outlier features above fp16 range, so the narrower storage is not an
option), and the extracted tensors run to ${\sim}210$\,MB per fp16 configuration and
${\sim}430$\,MB for the float32 one. The release therefore carries the two
pre-registrations, the generation and probing scripts, every per-step result JSON
for all three configurations, and the per-trial metadata including every
stored model response, but not the tensors, which re-extract from the released
scripts at the fixed seed (Appendix~\ref{app:probe_audit}).
\textbf{The second public release (EXP-XL) is gated, so we make no claim of
reproducibility parity with Apollo}: it can be neither vendored nor
submodule-pinned, and the path there is a committed sha256 manifest (revision,
per-file hash, byte size, verified row counts) plus every committed derived
artifact (eight per-configuration rule records, the eligibility survey, and
1{,}356 raw judgements) from which every EXP-XL number recomputes with no model
call and no network. \textbf{A manifest is weaker than a pin}: it lets a reader
detect that the corpus changed, not fetch the revision we audited. Accepting the
host's gate under one's own account is required to re-derive those artifacts from
raw rows, and is not required to reproduce any reported figure
(Appendix~\ref{app:liars_bench}).
\textbf{EXP-C4 (§\ref{sec:external_audit}) is local generation plus one grading pass.}
Its \emph{five} targets (Llama~3.2~3B, Llama~3.1~8B, Mistral~7B and
Qwen~2.5~7B/14B) are served locally under Ollama at the fixed seed. Qwen~2.5~32B is
excluded on one stated ground, recorded before any detector result was computed: its
belief screen could not be completed within the available memory, so we cannot show
it holds the negation of any reference fact (Appendix~\ref{app:crit4_ours}).
The only model call outside generation is the deception grader, and it is
\textbf{cross-family to every target}: \texttt{us.anthropic.claude-haiku-4-5-20251001-v1:0}
via AWS Bedrock at temperature~0, one call per trial, reading the on-claim answer
alone and never the probe vector the detector is scored on. Its 1{,}040 raw
judgements (757 confirmatory graded trials, the rest pilot) are committed as JSONL
(\texttt{data/results/crit4\_grades\_raw.jsonl}) alongside the rubric's md5, so
\textbf{every EXP-C4 number in the paper recomputes from the repository with no model
call}: H1--H4, the pilot's selection trace, the exploratory leave-one-target-out
transfer arm (\texttt{analyze\_crit4.py --phase transfer} $\to$
\texttt{data/results/crit4\_transfer.json}), and the grading reliability, whose 60
hand codes are literals in \texttt{experiments/analyze\_crit4\_reliability.py}.
\textbf{What the release contains.} Codebase, raw
transcripts, per-trial result files, and a table-reproduction script will be
released publicly, together with the \textbf{17 pre-registrations} cited by name
throughout this appendix, each frozen before its analysis code existed and each
carrying an append-only deviations log; the per-experiment analysis scripts named
at every results table; the committed result files from which every number
recomputes; the citation-verification script referenced in the AI use statement;
and a \textbf{2{,}765-check verification harness} asserting every load-bearing
number, every claim-to-experiment mapping, the page budget and the bibliography.
\textbf{Build identity.} Source revision~37, built 22~September~2026 from parent
commit \texttt{ea6faf9}. The revision number increments once per review round, so
a reader can check which round's tree a given PDF was compiled from.
