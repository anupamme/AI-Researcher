

We situate our contribution across three areas of prior work.

\subsection{White-Box Probes and Black-Box Behavioral Detection}

Deception-related information is encoded in model activations and extractable with weight access---honesty/deception directions~\citep{zou2023representation}, unsupervised truthfulness probes~\citep{burns2022discovering}, activation classifiers (SAPLMA;~\citealp{azaria2023internal}), inference-time steering (ITI;~\citealp{li2024inference})---all requiring access unavailable for API-only models. Unlike externally induced misbehavior~\citep{goodfellow2014explaining, perez2022ignore}, the model itself is the source of the deceptive output here.

\paragraph{Black-box behavioral approaches.} The closest prior work is \citet{pacchiardi2023catch}, who ask a fixed battery of \emph{unrelated} follow-up questions after a model states a possibly false claim and train a classifier on the binarised answers, finding detectable changes even on topically unrelated queries. We differ in three ways: (1)~our own instrument uses topically related, adaptive follow-ups; (2)~we add prompt-equalized and cross-family controls, under which most of the above-chance accuracy reported in this setting does not survive; and (3)~we give the identifiability analysis their fixed-condition evaluation leaves open, rather than a further detector. Their unrelated-question design leaves refusal-count accuracy nearly unchanged, consistent with the signal residing in the target's \emph{first} response (EXP-K, Appendix~\ref{app:pacchiardi}).

\paragraph{Instruction-following and judge controls.} \citet{sharma2023sycophancy} show LLMs shift answers toward perceived user preferences, controlling for it by comparing behavior with and without opinion-carrying context---the confound our prompt-equalized design targets, and we find the same pattern (93.9--100\% instructed $\to$ 52--69\% equalized under cross-family extraction). LLM evaluators also carry systematic biases, which motivates the cross-family control: \citet{verga2024replacing} find diverse judge panels more reliable than single judges, and here extractor choice moves accuracy by 9--16\,pp (EXP-H).

\subsection{Red-Teaming, Evaluation, and Autonomous Deception}

Red-teaming, human~\citep{ganguli2022red} and automated~\citep{perez2022red}, focuses on \emph{eliciting} harmful outputs rather than \emph{detecting} whether ostensibly normal outputs are deceptive---the complementary problem studied here.

\paragraph{Autonomous deception.} Deceptive behavior also arises without explicit instruction: strategic deception under pressure followed by misreporting~\citep{scheurer2024strategic}, alignment faking~\citep{greenblatt2024alignment}, learned reward tampering~\citep{denison2024sycophancy}, and capabilities that emerge with scale~\citep{hagendorff2024deception}. This regime matters most for safety and is the one our setting does not cover---it is also intervention~(A) of §\ref{sec:identification}, the manipulation that would identify the effect: a model deceiving autonomously need not produce the instruction-following behavior that instructed benchmarks cannot separate from deception.

\paragraph{Concurrent evaluations of lie detectors.} A recent line of work stress-tests lie detectors across settings rather than auditing one. \citet{kretschmar2025liarsbench} assemble 72{,}863 lie and honest-response examples over seven datasets and find black- and white-box techniques fail systematically on lie types not adjudicable from the transcript alone. \citet{cooney2026didyoulie} evaluate four detectors on 31 models (2B--1T) and 13 \emph{trained} organisms with verified hidden beliefs: all four scale positively on \emph{prompted} lying, while activation- and logprob-based detectors drop sharply on the organisms. \citet{hopkins2026liedetectors} report the same for fine-tuned detectors, at AUROC 0.95 in-distribution but 0.70--0.75 across held-out lie categories, barely beating prompted baselines. Those trained organisms instance intervention~(A)---deception varying with elicitation fixed---and the prompted-to-trained gap all three report is what the Proposition predicts: accuracy earned by manipulating the instruction carries no commitment about deception arising another way.

\paragraph{The requirements outside deception.} Group~(e) of Table~\ref{tab:prior_work_criteria} applies~(i)--(v) to four adjacent constructs, reading $D$ as the behavior each evaluation attributes its score to and \emph{the detector's input} as the channel that score is computed from. Every cell is scored from the published design alone; where an evaluation reports no per-response detector score, requirements about a detector's input are marked \emph{n/a} rather than guessed. Three of the four vary the elicitation to produce the score and so fail~(i): \citet{sharma2023sycophancy} insert the user's stated view or an ``are you sure?'' challenge, \citet{ganguli2022red} let each red-teamer choose and adapt their own attack, and \citet{greenblatt2024alignment} flip a free-tier flag inside the very input the alignment-faking grader also reads. The one design meeting all five is \citet{denison2024sycophancy}: its held-out environment holds the prompt fixed---``the prompt is always the same for this environment''---manipulates \emph{training} rather than the prompt, and grades the behavior by running the reward function and unit tests afterwards, so $D$ is grounded in program state rather than in the transcript a monitor would read. That is intervention~(A) of §\ref{sec:identification}, obtained in a setting where the behavior leaves a verifiable trace. \textbf{The verdicts differ, which is the point}: the requirements are decidable outside deception, and they separate designs that can attribute a behavior from designs that cannot---\citet{greenblatt2024alignment} name the corresponding gap themselves, that it ``is hard for us to verify'' what causes the behavioral difference persisting once the scratchpad is removed.

Two asymmetries with group~(a) are worth stating. \citet{sharma2023sycophancy} report the elicitation effect \emph{as} an elicitation effect and build no detector, so failing~(i) costs them nothing---the confound bites only when a design converts such materials into a detection accuracy. And \citet{ganguli2022red}'s attack-success label is supplied by the red-teamer who wrote the attack, with only ``poor to fair'' agreement in their own review task (Fleiss' $\kappa\!=\!0.32$); that is the annotation-channel problem of criterion~4 rather than the elicitation problem of criterion~1. \textbf{What group~(e) does not show} is that the elicitation confound is empirically \emph{active} in sycophancy, refusal, reward hacking or alignment faking: none of these four is a deception-detection benchmark, and establishing activity would need the equalization contrast we run only for deception (§\ref{sec:prompt_equalized}).

\paragraph{Summary of the gap.} These establish that lie detectors fail to \emph{generalize} across lie types, organisms, and training regimes. None isolates the identifiability problem created when the intervention that induces deception also induces instruction-following---so none answers whether the accuracy reported in the standard paradigm was ever attributable to deception. That is the question addressed here.

\textbf{Three questions, and only the third is ours.} The generalization studies ask \emph{does the detector transfer}, and answer no; the belief-verified organisms ask \emph{can a valid contrast be built}, and answer yes, going \emph{beyond} criterion~4 with $D$ \textbf{set} rather than observed (Table~\ref{tab:criteria}'s last row). Neither asks \emph{whether the published accuracy licensed the deception reading in the first place}---and that is a different question in two ways that matter. \textbf{It is decidable from a benchmark's description alone}: requirements~(i)--(v) are properties of a design, so an auditor who never runs the detector can settle them, which is how we decide them on ten designs in five groups, four of them outside deception (Table~\ref{tab:prior_work_criteria}). And \textbf{it is invisible in the benchmark's output}: the reported accuracy is the same number whether or not the criterion holds, so no amount of transfer testing, scale, or held-out evaluation surfaces it---a design that fails~(i) and one that satisfies it are indistinguishable from their metrics. This is why a generalization result cannot substitute for the audit: a detector could transfer perfectly across every lie type in \citet{kretschmar2025liarsbench} and the instructed contrast would still not identify $\tau_D$.

The constraint itself is a standard non-identifiability result, whose premises we state in full (§\ref{app:criteria_taxonomy}); what is new is enforcing it on a widely used paradigm, turning it into the five-criterion protocol of §\ref{sec:eval_controls}, and showing the failure is \emph{empirically active}---across three detector paradigms and both public releases---rather than a possibility raised and set aside.
