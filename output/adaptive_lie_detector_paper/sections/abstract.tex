\begin{abstract}
\textbf{The standard paradigm for evaluating behavioral LLM deception detectors conflates deception with instruction-following---and we show the conflation is active, not hypothetical.} A common evaluation paradigm scores such detectors in one setting, \emph{instructed roleplay}, where the model is told to lie; the instruction that produces the deception also produces the compliance, so a detector can score high while measuring whether the model was \emph{instructed} to lie. \textbf{Accuracy measured this way cannot establish deception-specific attribution or transfer beyond the instruction-induced regime}, and the reason is an identification failure: such a benchmark identifies $\tau_E$, the effect of the \emph{instruction} on the detector, whereas a claim that a detector detects deception \emph{as such} is a claim about $\tau_D$, the effect of \emph{deception}---and $\tau_E$ neither bounds nor signs $\tau_D$, so \textbf{no increase in accuracy, model scale, or detector sophistication resolves it unless the evaluation introduces an intervention that separates deception from instruction-following.} We turn this into a \textbf{falsifiable audit protocol}: five criteria required before accuracy licenses a claim about deception, four testable on any published benchmark and one that no instructed design can supply---and \textbf{the standard instructed-roleplay design cannot, by construction, satisfy it}.

Reimplemented from scratch, the standard black-box behavioral detector reaches 81.7\% on instructed-lie trials and \textbf{53.7\%}---no statistically detectable above-chance discrimination---once both conditions receive identical prompts (pooled and descriptive; the inference is per-target); a pre-registered replication on a disjoint claim set and an independently worded probe bank then \textbf{eliminates above-chance discrimination for all six targets} (97.0\%$\to$\textbf{43.3\%}), two mechanistically unrelated detectors failing under one control. A parameter-free lexical rule reaches \textbf{69--80\%}---\emph{surface-accessible accuracy}, reachable from a hand-specified feature with no deception-specific modelling, and not a decomposition of the detector's signal. Finally we audit \textbf{ten rollout sets from one public release} for the one criterion that removes the elicitation confound, namely deception varying at \emph{fixed} elicitation: \textbf{nine cannot supply it for our deployed-observable rule}, and on the one that can, the rule is \textbf{null} ($+4.1$/$+7.7$\,pp, $p\!\geq\!0.17$); scoring its \emph{hidden} scaffold instead reports $+84.3$\,pp---the model echoing ``incorrect'' from its own instruction---so \textbf{a benchmark must expose only the channel a deployed detector could read}. We claim identification, not attribution: these detectors \emph{can} classify prompted deception and we reproduce it, but instructed-roleplay accuracy cannot establish deception as the \emph{cause} of the signal---\textbf{a failure of attribution for the canonical instructed paradigm, not a failure of behavioral deception detection in general}. \textbf{Equalization may remove the deception with the confound}; criterion~4, not the collapse, is where attribution is tested, and \textbf{one corpus with independently validated deception at fixed elicitation would overturn us in a single experiment}---the falsifier we most want, and none of the ten supplies it. \textbf{Scope: English, instructed-roleplay evaluations, open-weight models 3B--70B.}
\end{abstract}
