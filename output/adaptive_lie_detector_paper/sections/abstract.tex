\begin{abstract}
\textbf{We do not ask whether LLMs can detect deception; we ask whether current benchmarks provide evidence that they do.} \textbf{The standard paradigm for evaluating behavioral LLM deception detectors conflates deception with instruction-following---and we show the conflation is active, not hypothetical.} A common paradigm scores such detectors in one setting, \emph{instructed roleplay}: the model is told to lie, and the instruction that produces the deception also produces the compliance, so a detector can score high while measuring whether the model was \emph{instructed} to lie. \textbf{Accuracy measured this way cannot establish deception-specific attribution or transfer beyond the instruction-induced regime}, and the reason is an identification failure: such a benchmark identifies $\tau_E$, the \emph{instruction}'s effect on the detector, whereas detecting deception \emph{as such} is a claim about $\tau_D$, the effect of \emph{deception}---and $\tau_E$ neither bounds nor signs $\tau_D$, so \textbf{no increase in accuracy, model scale, or detector sophistication resolves it unless the evaluation introduces an intervention that separates deception from instruction-following.} We turn this into a \textbf{falsifiable audit protocol}: five criteria required before accuracy licenses a deception claim---three alternative-explanation diagnostics, one construct-validity test that \textbf{no instructed design can supply}, and one robustness test.

Reimplemented from scratch, the standard black-box behavioral detector reaches 81.7\% on instructed-lie trials and \textbf{53.7\%}---no detectable above-chance discrimination---once both conditions receive identical prompts (pooled and descriptive; the inference is per-target); a pre-registered replication on a disjoint claim set and an independently worded probe bank then \textbf{eliminates above-chance discrimination for all six targets} (97.0\%$\to$\textbf{43.3\%}), two mechanistically unrelated detectors failing under one control---as does \textbf{a white-box linear probe on residual activations in two model families}, at 100.0\% instructed but transferring to equalized responses at chance and matched by surface features (92.5--99.0\%). A parameter-free lexical rule reaches \textbf{69--80\%}---\emph{surface-accessible accuracy}, with no deception-specific modelling, and not a decomposition of the detector's signal. Finally we audit \textbf{both public rollout releases} for the one criterion that removes the elicitation confound, deception varying at \emph{fixed} elicitation: of ten rollout sets \textbf{nine cannot supply it for a deployed-observable rule}; on the one that can, our rule is \textbf{null} ($+4.1$/$+7.7$\,pp, $p\!\geq\!0.17$) while \textbf{the corpus authors' own black-box judge, run verbatim on the same visible text, does separate deception there} (AUROC 0.71/0.94, $p\!=\!0.0001$), so the null is the instrument's, not the criterion's; scoring the \emph{hidden} scaffold instead reports $+84.3$\,pp---\textbf{annotation-channel leakage}, the model echoing ``incorrect'' from its own instruction---so \textbf{a benchmark must expose only the channel a deployed detector could read}. \textbf{A second release---35 cells, five generator families, fully pre-registered---fails differently}: its label grades the very text both instruments read, so passing is \textbf{construct recovery}. We claim identification, not attribution: these detectors \emph{can} classify prompted deception and we reproduce it---\textbf{a failure of attribution for the canonical paradigm, not of behavioral deception detection in general}. \textbf{Equalization may remove the deception with the confound}; criterion~4, not the collapse, is where attribution is tested, and \textbf{one corpus with independently validated deception at fixed elicitation would overturn us in a single experiment}---the falsifier we most want, and neither release supplies all five. \textbf{Scope: English, instructed-roleplay evaluations, open-weight models 3B--70B.}
\end{abstract}
