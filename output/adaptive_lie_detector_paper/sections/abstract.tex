\begin{abstract}
\textbf{Accuracy on instructed-lie benchmarks does not identify deception: the intervention that creates the lie also changes instruction-following behavior.} Such benchmarks are validated by predictive accuracy, not by construct identification. We introduce an identification audit for them, and show that the common instructed-lie paradigm fails it. \textbf{We do not ask whether LLMs can detect deception; we ask whether current benchmarks provide evidence that they do.} The standard paradigm scores detectors in one setting, \emph{instructed roleplay}, so a detector can score high while measuring whether the model was \emph{instructed} to lie. The reason is structural: \textbf{no experiment that intervenes only on the instruction can separate a detector response mediated by deception from one mediated by instruction-following}, both being descendants of that instruction. A deception-attribution reading therefore needs $\tau_D$, the effect of \emph{deception}, which the identified $\tau_E$---the \emph{instruction}'s effect---neither bounds nor signs, so no gain in accuracy, scale, or detector sophistication resolves it. \textbf{This is an identification problem, not a generalization problem: generalization can be perfect while identification still fails.} We turn it into a falsifiable audit protocol: a five-part identification audit---\textbf{one necessary construct-validity test no instructed design can supply}, plus three alternative-explanation diagnostics and one robustness test. Fixed elicitation itself is not new---trained belief-verified organisms have it---but it has not been used as the \emph{construct-validity criterion} for reading instructed-benchmark accuracy.

Reimplemented from scratch, the standard black-box behavioral detector reaches 81.7\% on instructed-lie trials and 53.7\% once both conditions receive identical prompts. A pre-registered replication on a disjoint claim set and probe bank then \textbf{leaves no detectable above-chance discrimination on any of the six targets} (97.0\%$\to$\textbf{43.3\%}), as does a white-box linear probe on residual activations in two model families, at 100.0\% instructed, at chance equalized, and matched by surface features (92.5--99.0\%). \textbf{The accuracy the benchmark is scored on is reachable with no learned features at all}: a parameter-free lexical rule reaches 69--80\%---\emph{surface-accessible accuracy}, not a decomposition of the detector's signal. We then audit both public rollout releases for the one criterion that removes the elicitation confound: \textbf{nine of ten rollout sets cannot supply it} for a deployed-observable rule; on the tenth our rule is \textbf{null} while \textbf{the corpus authors' own judge, run verbatim on the same visible text, does separate deception there} (AUROC 0.71/0.94), so the null is the instrument's, not the criterion's; scoring the \emph{hidden} scaffold instead reports $+84.3$\,pp---\textbf{annotation-channel leakage}, the model echoing ``incorrect'' from its own instruction. A second release---35 cells, five generator families, fully pre-registered---fails differently: its label grades the very text both instruments read, so passing is \textbf{construct recovery}. We claim identification, not attribution, and \textbf{equalization may remove the deception with the confound}. Because \textbf{no public release we audited supplies all five}, we built materials that do: with deception operationalized independently of the detector's input, prior work's battery separates operationally verified deception on three of five targets---all three with power ($p\!\leq\!0.006$; two underpowered nulls)---of which \textbf{a blinded re-run replicates two and a pre-registered covariate audit then withdraws one of those two, so the standing count is one of five}, with \textbf{one of five \emph{new} targets positive}. \textbf{The audit also changes \emph{which} probe indicates deception}: the instructed benchmark's most discriminative question is inert at fixed elicitation. \textbf{The canonical benchmark fails to identify deception, while a fixed-elicitation design reveals \emph{target-dependent} deception-associated signals}---still not $\tau_D$---\textbf{rung~4 of five}. \textbf{Scope: English, instructed-roleplay evaluations, open-weight models 3B--70B.}
\end{abstract}
