\section{Introduction}

Behavioral deception detection in LLMs almost always reports accuracies in a single setting, \emph{instructed roleplay}. This paper asks whether the resulting performance reflects detection of deception itself, or merely artifacts of the instruction to deceive --- and finds that existing detectors do not yet separate the two.

An audit of this kind invites an obvious objection: an artifact found in the auditors' own reimplementation may say more about the reimplementation than about the paradigm. We therefore begin with prior work's detector rather than our own. A from-scratch reimplementation of the published mechanism of \citet{pacchiardi2023catch} --- fixed unrelated yes/no elicitation questions, binary-coded answers, logistic regression --- reproduces accuracy in the same broad range under the instructed protocol and drops it to chance under a single control that equalizes the prompts (§\ref{sec:r1_faithful}). The confound belongs to the paradigm, not to one implementation.

This paper is a \textbf{methodological audit and causal analysis} of behavioral LLM deception-detection benchmarks (from-scratch reimplementation throughout; original code, data, and question sets were not obtained). Three evaluation diagnostics --- \textbf{prompt equalization} (identical neutral prompts, removing the deception instruction), \textbf{cross-family extraction} (extractors from a different model family), and \textbf{surface-level baselines} (regex classifiers) --- quantify how much reported accuracy survives each control. A 2$\times$2 factorial then supplies the causal test (§\ref{sec:factorial}): after independently manipulating truth-value and deception instruction, does a deception-specific signal survive? We evaluate seven LLMs (Llama~3.2~3B, Llama~3.1~8B, Mistral~7B, Qwen~2.5~7B/14B, Llama~3.3~70B, Claude Haiku~4.5), all $\leq$70B. The empirical findings are:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{The prior detector, reimplemented from scratch, also collapses under equalization.} Across six targets the mechanism of \citet{pacchiardi2023catch} yields 70.0--100.0\% instructed (bracketing their 67--73\%); four of five fall to chance under equalization (48.0--52.0\%, $p\!\geq\!0.468$; pooled 81.7\%$\to$53.7\%). Qwen~2.5~14B is a documented exception at 70.0\% ($p\!=\!0.015$), consistent with a confidence probe tracking knowledge conflict (§\ref{sec:r1_faithful}). Two mechanistically unrelated detectors fail under the same control.
    \item \textbf{A 2$\times$2 factorial shows neither detector establishes deception-specific detection independent of instruction-induced behavior.} Equalization alone is ambiguous --- the instruction may be a confound, or equalization may simply remove deception. Independently manipulating truth-value and deception instruction resolves this: both detectors strongly encode the instruction factor, while the complete detector's signed response to the instruction is large and consistent ($+1.3$ to $+1.6$\,SD) and its response to claim content changes sign --- establishing that the benchmark cannot separate deception from its elicitation. Grouped-by-claim cross-validation confirms the same picture for the binary-probe LR. A 12-variant paraphrase test on two models (§\ref{sec:paraphrase_invariance}) finds the rule below chance on essentially every variant (35--51\%); the LR fails instruction-invariance in opposite directions across the two (IIS $=0.938$ vs.\ $0.367$), neither of which is consistent with deception detection.
    \item \textbf{Generalization is weak, and the residual signal is achievable from surface lexical behavior.} Leave-one-model-out: \textbf{64.2\%} [CI: 59--69\%]; leave-one-claim-pair-out: \textbf{67.4\%} [CI: 64.6--70.3\%] --- withholding an entire claim pair changes accuracy by only 0.3\,pp, so the result is not claim memorization. A hand-specified refusal-count regex reaches \textbf{69--80\%} depending on pattern choice (79.8\% for the full 14-pattern set), bracketing the pipeline and showing the residual does not establish deception-specific representation. Four of the five behavioral features fail inter-rater reliability ($\alpha\!<\!0.4$); even under the most defensible human-rated operationalization, accuracy is only \textbf{54.5\%} [CI: 40--67\%] at 0.3\% benign FPR.
    \item \textbf{Transfer beyond instructed roleplay is weak or null (exploratory).} Sycophancy retains above-chance separation (68.5--83\%), but most is already present under the system-prompt-only control ($+$5--14\,pp marginal); persona and false-belief are null (Appendix~\ref{app:autonomous_transfer}).
\end{enumerate}

Our contribution is not a new detector. It is a \textbf{causal evaluation protocol} for determining whether a behavioral deception detector measures deception rather than the instruction to deceive.


\paragraph{Scope.} We study \textbf{instructed roleplay on open-weight models (3B--70B)}, English only, not autonomous strategic deception~\cite{hubinger2019risks, scheurer2024strategic, greenblatt2024alignment}. Both the rule and the pipeline read the lexical surface and degrade under adversarial paraphrase (§\ref{sec:adversarial_paraphrase}).
