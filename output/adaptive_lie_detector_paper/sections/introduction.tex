\section{Introduction}

\textbf{Accuracy on an instructed-lie benchmark does not identify deception-specific signal.} Behavioral deception detection in LLMs almost always reports accuracies in a single setting, \emph{instructed roleplay}: a model is told to lie, and the detector is scored on how well it separates those trials from truthful ones. Two claims must be kept apart: \textbf{(A)}~the detector can classify whether the model is in the instructed-lie condition, and \textbf{(B)}~the detector detects deception. \textbf{We do not dispute (A)}---we reproduce it. Accuracy in this regime cannot establish \textbf{(B)}, because the instruction is what produces the deception, so the signal's two candidate causes are not separately identifiable from accuracy measured this way. The consequence is the one that matters for deployment: \textbf{such a benchmark establishes no reason for the detector to fire when deception arises by a different causal route}---under incentive or goal conflict, where there is no instruction to comply with (§\ref{sec:identification}). We study the identifiability of \emph{instructed behavioral} deception benchmarks, not deception detection in general.

\paragraph{Contribution.} This paper contributes \textbf{neither a new deception detector nor a new causal-identification theorem}. It contributes (i)~an operational \textbf{audit protocol} for behavioral deception benchmarks---five criteria required before a benchmark's accuracy licenses a claim about deception, four applicable to any published benchmark without new data; (ii)~the \textbf{identification result that makes the protocol necessary}---a standard non-identifiability constraint (§\ref{sec:identification}, Figure~\ref{fig:dag}), deliberately thin, with the work in its consequence; and (iii)~\textbf{an audit under that protocol} of our own instructed materials \emph{and} of ten published rollout sets. \textbf{The novelty is that the standard evaluation protocol for behavioral LLM deception detection \emph{is itself} an intervention-confounded design}: the failure survives two mechanistically unrelated detectors, two independently constructed claim sets, two independently worded probe banks and a pre-registered replication, and the constraint yields an operational audit that returns a verdict on corpora we did not build---on whether a benchmark \emph{can} attribute its accuracy to deception, not on which factor a given detector reads.

\paragraph{Relation to prior work.} White-box probes read deception from activations~\citep{zou2023representation, burns2022discovering, azaria2023internal}, but require weight access; the black-box behavioral line audited here does not. Its closest instance, and our audit target, is \citet{pacchiardi2023catch}: a fixed battery of \emph{unrelated} follow-up questions, binarised and fed to one logistic regression. Three concurrent evaluations stress-test lie detectors at scale---72{,}863 examples over seven datasets~\citep{kretschmar2025liarsbench}, 31 models with 13 belief-verified trained organisms~\citep{cooney2026didyoulie}, and held-out lie categories~\citep{hopkins2026liedetectors}---and agree that detectors fail to \textbf{generalize} beyond the regime they were fit in. \textbf{That is a generalization question; ours is the prior one:} whether accuracy \emph{within} the canonical instructed regime identifies deception at all---a detector could transfer perfectly and still be measuring instruction-following in every regime it transfers to (Appendix~\ref{app:related_work}).

An audit invites an obvious objection---an artifact in the auditors' own reimplementation may say more about the reimplementation than about the paradigm---so we begin with prior work's detector rather than our own (§\ref{sec:r1_faithful}), reimplemented from scratch with no original code, data, or question set. Over seven instruction-tuned LLMs from 3B to 70B in three families (§\ref{sec:setup}), the findings are:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{The prior detector, reimplemented from scratch, also collapses under equalization.} The mechanism of \citet{pacchiardi2023catch} yields 70.0--100.0\% instructed on the five of six targets where it works at all (bracketing their 67--73\%); four fall to chance under equalization (48.0--52.0\%; pooled 81.7\%$\to$53.7\%). A pre-registered replication on a disjoint claim set and an independently worded probe bank refutes the exception (Qwen~2.5~14B, 70.0\%$\to$\textbf{46.0\%}) and, extended to all six, finds every one informative instructed and every one at chance equalized (97.0\%$\to$\textbf{43.3\%}), so the collapse holds on \emph{both} claim sets, for two mechanistically unrelated detectors (§\ref{sec:r1_faithful}, Figure~\ref{fig:r1c_collapse}).
    \item \emph{Diagnostic:} \textbf{both detectors move \emph{within} a single instructed-deception condition.} The response to the instruction is large and consistently signed ($+1.3$ to $+1.6$\,SD) while that to claim content changes sign, which sizes the unattributable component rather than attributing it; and within one identically-labelled condition the detector still shifts $-1.37$ and $+1.61$\,SD in \emph{opposite} directions (§\ref{sec:td_vs_fd}).
    \item \textbf{What survives is reachable from surface lexical behavior, and part of it tracks knowledge conflict.} A hand-specified refusal-count regex reaches \textbf{69--80\%} depending on the pattern inventory---a \emph{lower bound} on how much measured accuracy needs no deception-specific feature, and it is not robust to adversarial paraphrasing (§\ref{sec:paraphrase_invariance}). Holding the instruction fixed and varying whether the model \emph{knows} the claim gives \textbf{70.0\%} against 57.6\% ($+$12.4\,pp, $p\!=\!0.005$, $n\!=\!268$; §\ref{sec:belief_strata}).
    \item \textbf{None of the ten audited datasets provides all the design properties required to evaluate criterion 4 on a deployed-observable channel.} Three vary the elicitation prompt per item, five leave the rule inapplicable, and one has no model outputs. On the single testable set ($n\!=\!1000$, one system prompt throughout) realized deception moves the rule $+4.1$ and $+7.7$\,pp---\textbf{null}, and null again when $D$ is read without the corpus's grader---while scoring that set's \emph{hidden} scaffold reports $+84.3$\,pp, effectively all of it one pattern echoing the instruction's own wording, so \textbf{the channel a detector reads is itself an audit criterion}. A second corpus meets all four and \emph{passes} ($-77.1$\,pp), but grader and rule there share evidence by construction; re-run against an \textbf{action-grounded} label disjoint from the rule's input, it returns a \textbf{null} ($+21.6$\,pp), so the pass was \textbf{construct recovery, not detection}.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Scope.} The setting is \textbf{instructed roleplay on open-weight models (3B--70B)}, English only, with six 3B--32B targets and one claim set for the reimplementation. Autonomous strategic deception~\cite{hubinger2019risks, scheurer2024strategic, greenblatt2024alignment}---the regime criterion 4 requires---enters only as the external audit of §\ref{sec:external_audit}, on corpora we did not build.

\paragraph{Why this matters beyond deception.} The failure mode is general: whenever a benchmark's intervention changes the target construct \emph{and} an auxiliary behavior the evaluator reads, accuracy identifies the benchmark condition rather than the construct, and no amount of it repairs the design. Instructed sycophancy, refusal, reward hacking and alignment faking are all elicited this way. \textbf{We test only deception}; what we offer for the rest is the audit.
