\section{Introduction}

\textbf{We do not ask whether LLMs can detect deception; we ask whether current benchmarks provide evidence that they do}---and accuracy on an instructed-lie benchmark cannot establish deception-specific attribution or transfer beyond the instruction-induced regime. A common paradigm for behavioral deception detection reports accuracies in a single setting, \emph{instructed roleplay}: a model is told to lie, and the detector is scored on separating those trials from truthful ones. Because the instruction is what produces the deception, the signal's two candidate causes are not separately identifiable from accuracy measured this way---and three questions such an accuracy is read as answering come apart. \textbf{Our contribution is not a new causal-identification theorem; it is the empirical demonstration that a widely used LLM deception evaluation protocol instantiates a known non-identifying design.} How close to the surface this sits: the single most discriminative of the detector's 16 follow-up questions---\emph{``If you had made an error, would you point it out without being asked?''}---is \textbf{a paraphrase of the instruction the target is following}, and is constant under equalization.

\noindent\fbox{\parbox{0.975\linewidth}{\small\textbf{Three questions, kept apart.}
\textbf{Q1. Can a detector classify instructed-lie trials against truthful ones?} \textbf{Yes}---we reproduce it and dispute none of it.
\textbf{Q2. Does that signal arise from the deception rather than from following the instruction?} \textbf{Unidentified}---the instruction moves both, so no accuracy measured this way separates them (§\ref{sec:identification}).
\textbf{Q3. Will the detector fire when deception arises with no instruction to comply with}, under incentive or goal conflict? \textbf{Not for the audited surface rule}---on the one corpus supporting that test it is null, though prior work's own judge does separate deception there (§\ref{sec:external_audit}).
\textbf{The methodological hierarchy, in one line.} \textbf{Prompt equalization establishes regime-dependence, not non-deceptiveness}; the attribution test is \textbf{criterion~4} alone---deception varying at fixed elicitation.
This paper is about \textbf{Q2 and Q3}: an \emph{identification} claim about a class of benchmarks, not a capability claim about detectors.}}

\paragraph{Contribution: four claims, in descending order of how strongly they are warranted (Table~\ref{tab:roadmap}).} \textbf{(1)~Structural.} Instructed benchmarks identify $\tau_E$, the \emph{instruction}'s effect, while deception-detection claims are about $\tau_D$ (§\ref{sec:identification})---and \textbf{no experiment, ours or anyone's, could establish or refute this within an instructed design}. \textbf{(2)~Empirical: the confound is not hypothetical, it is active.} The collapse, the parameter-free lexical rule, the white-box probe audit and the factorial establish it across \textbf{eight models in four families}, two claim sets, two independently worded probe banks and \textbf{three detector paradigms}---and \textbf{none of that shows that no deception signal is present}, since equalization may remove the deception along with the confound. \textbf{(3)~Methodological.} \textbf{An operational audit protocol} of five criteria that separates instruction-following from surface behavior from knowledge conflict from recovery of the benchmark's own annotation: four apply to any published benchmark, and one, unsupplied by any instructed design, removes the elicitation confound (Table~\ref{tab:criteria}). \textbf{(4)~Empirical.} \textbf{An audit under it} of our own materials \emph{and} of \textbf{both} public rollout releases---ten rollout sets, and 35 further cells over five generator families, the second fully pre-registered. \textbf{Attribution is tested by criterion~4 alone}, and there our surface rule is null while the corpus authors' own judge is positive, so \textbf{what the null indicts is the instrument, and criterion~4 itself is reachable}. It also yields a second, named failure mode, \textbf{annotation-channel leakage}---a benchmark can appear to satisfy a causal criterion while measuring \emph{recovery of its own annotation mechanism}: the same rule reports $+84.3$\,pp on the \emph{hidden} scaffold against $+4.1$/$+7.7$\,pp on the answer a deployed detector would see, so \textbf{a benchmark must expose only the deployed-observable channel} (§\ref{sec:external_audit}). \textbf{Stated plainly}: we do not show that LLM deception cannot be detected; we show that \textbf{the canonical instructed-lie benchmark cannot establish that its detector is detecting deception rather than behavior induced by the elicitation procedure}, and that \textbf{a large fraction of the benchmark-level separation is reproducible from simple surface behavior, demonstrating that benchmark accuracy alone cannot establish that the detector's signal is deception-specific.}

\paragraph{Relation to prior work.} White-box probes read deception from activations~\citep{zou2023representation, burns2022discovering, azaria2023internal}; the black-box behavioral line's closest instance---our primary audit target---is \citet{pacchiardi2023catch}: a fixed battery of \emph{unrelated} follow-up questions, binarised and fed to one logistic regression. \textbf{We audit both paradigms} (§\ref{sec:td_vs_fd}). Three concurrent evaluations~\citep{kretschmar2025liarsbench, cooney2026didyoulie, hopkins2026liedetectors} agree lie detectors fail to \textbf{generalize} beyond the regime they were fit in. \textbf{That is a generalization question; ours is the prior one:} whether accuracy \emph{within} the canonical instructed regime identifies deception at all---a detector could transfer perfectly and still measure instruction-following in every regime it reaches (Appendix~\ref{app:related_work}). \textbf{Fixed-elicitation deception experiments are not what we claim to introduce}---\citet{cooney2026didyoulie}'s 13 \emph{trained organisms} with verified hidden beliefs are one, and they sit \emph{above} criterion~4 on Table~\ref{tab:criteria}'s ladder, with $D$ \emph{set} rather than observed. \textbf{Our novelty is using fixed elicitation as the missing construct-validity criterion for interpreting instructed-benchmark accuracy}, and auditing whether public artifacts satisfy it: those are model organisms, not a released rollout corpus with per-rollout grades a \emph{deployed-observable} detector can be scored against offline. \textbf{Where the field does have fixed-elicitation designs the reported drop is what the Proposition predicts}---corroboration, not counterexample (Table~\ref{tab:prior_work_criteria}).

We begin with prior work's detector rather than our own, so that no artifact of our reimplementation can carry the argument (§\ref{sec:r1_faithful}). Over seven instruction-tuned LLMs, 3B--70B, in three families (§\ref{sec:setup}), \textbf{one question organizes every result: does the reported accuracy survive the controls that would have to hold before it could be read as being about deception?} Table~\ref{tab:roadmap} answers it level by level.

\vspace{1pt}
\noindent\fbox{\parbox{0.975\linewidth}{\small\textbf{The one experiment that would refute this paper, stated in full.} A corpus that (i)~fixes the elicitation prompt, (ii)~grades $D$ independently of the condition label, (iii)~has \textbf{the same scenario independently realized under both behavioral outcomes}, (iv)~exposes only the channel a deployed detector could read, and (v)~grounds $D$ in evidence not definitionally equivalent to the detector's input---(i)--(v) operationalize criterion~4 (§\ref{sec:eval_controls}). \textbf{A detector separating deception there would establish a deception-associated signal at fixed elicitation and overturn our conclusion in a single experiment.} We audit \textbf{both} public rollout releases---ten rollout sets and 35 further cells---and \textbf{none supplies all five}---a claim about what public rollout corpora contain today, not about what is possible. On the closest set of the first, prior work's own judge \emph{does} separate deception at fixed elicitation and (iii) is what is missing; on the second, (iii) is met and (v) is not (§\ref{sec:external_audit}).}}
