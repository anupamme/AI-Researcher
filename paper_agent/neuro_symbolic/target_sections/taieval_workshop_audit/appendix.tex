\section{The Superseded $\mathrm{score}_5$ Comparison, in Full}
\label{app:retraction}

Our first attempt at the leaderboard comparison of \S\ref{sec:leaderboard} computed
$\mathrm{score}_5$ with the \textsc{UnseenEqClass} split serving as its own neighbour pool, and
normalised by $k$. Under that procedure a bag or an untrained encoder matched or beat the StructEmb
ablation on 13 of 14 corpora, beat the headline SemEmb system on 3, and reached $69.4$ on
\textsc{Bool8} where EqNet reports $58.1$. Every difference from the published procedure pushed our
numbers up:

\begin{enumerate}[noitemsep,topsep=2pt,leftmargin=1.4em]
  \item \emph{Neighbour pool.} \citet{allamanis2017learning} embed the entire corpus and search
    neighbours in it; the test split only supplies queries. On \textsc{Bool8} that is $257{,}784$
    candidate neighbours rather than $13{,}859$. More distractors can only lower a score.
  \item \emph{Normalisation.} Their score divides by $\min(|\text{class}|, k)$, the number of
    same-class neighbours actually achievable, not by $k$.
  \item \emph{Query set.} Their queries are corpus entries whose token string appears in the test
    split, so each query sits in the pool alongside the rest of its class.
\end{enumerate}

Reproducing all three moves the comparison from ``matches or beats StructEmb on 13 of 14'' to 5 of
14, and from ``beats SemEmb on 3'' to none. We keep the superseded numbers on record rather than
deleting them.

\section{What the Separability Screen Does Not Predict}
\label{app:screen}

The auditor's separability columns measure whether class \emph{representatives} differ; they do not
predict what a baseline scores on \emph{held-out forms}, and the two diverge sharply. Across 12
corpora no zero-parameter separability statistic we tried predicts untrained-encoder accuracy
($R^2 = -2.8$): the statistic saturates while accuracy does not. We report this as a limitation of
the tool rather than tuning a statistic until it correlated. The operational consequence is step~2
of the checklist: run both baselines rather than reading a projection off the screen.

Relatedly, the two baseline families measure different things and neither substitutes for the
other. On EQNET \textsc{oneVarPoly13} the bag scores $0.15$ where the untrained encoder scores
$0.66$; on Feynman-10 the ordering reverses ($1.00$ versus $0.13$). A recursive nonlinearity is
sensitive to tree shape a bag discards, and blind to count differences a bag sees.

\section{Per-Equation Heterogeneity}
\label{app:units}

Identification accuracy on a $K$-equation suite is usually reported as a mean over seeds with a
seed-level interval. That answers ``would a rerun give this number?'' when the question of interest
is ``would other equations give this number?'' The two differ substantially here: per-equation
leakage on the 10-equation suite ranges from $-0.05$ (energy density) to $1.12$ (wavenumber, whose
held-out form is a training duplicate: trivially solvable, so renaming destroys the only cue).
Resampling equations rather than seeds widens the interval by roughly $6\times$. We report
equation-level bootstraps throughout and recommend that any benchmark with fewer than a few dozen
classes do the same; with $K{=}10$ a seed-level interval is close to meaningless as a statement
about the task.
